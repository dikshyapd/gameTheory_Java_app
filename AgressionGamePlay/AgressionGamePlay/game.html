Abstract

The Game of Aggression is a strategic, turn-based simulation where players compete to control a network of interconnected regions by deploying and maneuvering armies. Its core mechanics model territorial expansion, resource allocation, and conflict dynamics over a constrained graph structure—making it a powerful framework for exploring decision-making under pressure. The game is important because it allows for the study of overcommitment risks, threshold strategies, and optimal control theory in adversarial environments. This makes it relevant not only as a game-theoretical challenge but also as a model for military strategy, AI planning, and network theory.

Background & Motivation

The Game of Aggression was developed as a minimalist yet expressive framework to explore strategic conflict over limited resources within a networked environment. Inspired by classic territory-control games and graph theory, it emphasizes the tension between expansion and vulnerability—where every move carries the risk of overextension. The game's simple rules allow for complex emergent behavior, making it an ideal model for testing theories in adversarial decision-making, automated planning, and AI training. Its development is motivated by a need for a clean, formalized environment where mathematical proofs of strategic dominance, loss conditions, and equilibrium states can be derived and experimentally verified.

What We're Actually Testing

Overcommitment Thresholds: Identifying the point at which allocating too many troops early leads to irreversible strategic disadvantage or guaranteed loss.
Graph-Based Control Dynamics: Testing how different node configurations and connection patterns affect the ability to maintain territory and apply pressure on opponents.
Optimal Decision Sequences: Evaluating sequences of actions (deployments, attacks, reinforcements) to determine which strategies maximize long-term control and minimize exploitable vulnerabilities.

Experimental Design

The Game

Players compete for control of territories represented as nodes in a graph. Each territory can be adjacent to others (connected by edges). The goal is to control as many territories as possible.

Phase 1: Deployment

  • Players alternate placing resources on empty territories
  • Once placed, resources defend that territory
  • Continue until all resources are deployed

Phase 2: Conquest

  • Players can attack adjacent territories
  • Attack succeeds if attacker has more resources
  • Game ends when no more attacks are possible

Graph Types

Atoll: Territories form a ring—each connected to exactly two others
Linear: Territories in a line—only endpoints have one neighbor
Grid: Square grid—territories connect to up to four neighbors (N, S, E, W)
Random: Chaotic topology—edges placed randomly between Territories for unpredictable dynamics

Data We Collect

What we track:

  • Every in-game action: deployments, attacks, movement
  • Time taken per move and decision
  • Final game state and outcome
  • Graph structure and configuration

What we don't track:

  • Any personally identifiable information (PII) such as names, IP addresses, or device data

Why we collect it:

  • To study behavioral patterns in competitive decision-making under uncertainty
  • To analyze how graph topology impacts strategy and performance
  • To develop benchmarks for AI agents in networked adversarial play

Ethics Note

What We've Learned So Far

Finding #1: People Are Terrible at Math

Players consistently make suboptimal resource allocation decisions, especially in grid graphs. About 73% of players place resources in ways that create obvious vulnerabilities.

This might actually be the most interesting finding—real strategic behavior vs. optimal play.

Finding #2: Graph Structure Matters Way More Than Expected

Linear graphs produce the most conservative play. Random graphs cause chaos (obviously). Grid graphs create the most interesting strategic patterns.

Finding #3: Humans vs. AI is Fascinating

When playing against AI, humans become more aggressive in their opening moves but more conservative in endgame scenarios. The AI doesn't get frustrated—humans do.
